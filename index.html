What you'll learn

By the end of this course you should be able to:

  • apply materials to the bodies in your design
  • add decals, change the environment and render photo-realistic images of your model
  • produce an animation and a presentation of your design using CAD tools

What is rendering?

Rendering means a computer program turns your design into a finished image. It combines everything it knows about the scene: the shape of the model (its geometry), the camera position, the lighting, and the textures and materials on each part.

Before you render, you give each part of your design an appearance material, such as plastic, glass, metal, paint or wood. These materials hold the visual properties that make the final image look photo-realistic, so you can see how your design would look in the real world.

The Render workspace in Fusion

Fusion keeps its rendering tools in their own Render workspace, which has two main areas.

The Setup section is where you prepare the scene:

  • Appearance: choose the materials for each part
  • Scene Settings: set the environment, such as lighting and background
  • Decal: place images, like logos or labels, onto surfaces

The Render section is where you start and stop rendering.

Physical vs. appearance materials

Fusion gives every part two kinds of material, and each one does a different job.

A physical material describes what the object is actually made of. Fusion uses it to work out properties like mass. If you haven't applied an appearance, the part is shown in its default physical material. You can change that default in your user preferences.

An appearance material only controls how the object looks when it's rendered. It doesn't change the mass or other engineering properties.

Types of appearance

Fusion groups its appearance materials into a few families, based on how they handle light:

  • Opaque: materials you can't see through, or that let only a little light through (translucent). Examples are plastic, marble, wood, and liquids like milk.
  • Transparent: materials you can see through, like glass, some plastics and most liquids.
  • Metal: metallic materials, which are very shiny and give strong, sharp reflections.
  • Layered: coated materials, such as carbon fibre and metallic car paint.
  • Wood (Solid): a 3D wood material with grain that runs all the way through, so a cut in any direction still looks like real wood.

Appearance: colour

There are two common ways to choose a colour for a material.

RGB describes a colour as a mix of red, green and blue light. It's how screens show colour.

Pantone (also called PMS, the Pantone Matching System) is a standard library of numbered colours that's used around the world. It was created so printers and designers could agree on exactly which colour to print. It also includes colours that can't be mixed from the four standard printing inks (CMYK). Picking a Pantone colour means your render can match the real product.

Appearance: roughness

Up close, every real surface is made of countless tiny reflective facets, each pointing in a slightly different direction. From a distance, these facets combine into the highlight or reflection you see on the surface.

The roughness setting copies this effect. On a smooth surface the facets all line up, which gives a sharp, mirror-like reflection. On a rough surface they point in many directions, so the reflection spreads out and looks soft or matte.

Further reading: Surface reflectance settings (Graphisoft help)

Appearance: index of refraction

Light travels at different speeds through different materials. When it passes from one material into another, such as from air into glass, it slows down and bends. This bending is called refraction.

How much a material slows light down is given as a number called its index of refraction. The higher the number, the more light bends. It's a key setting for making glass, water and other transparent materials look right.

Appearance: reflectance

A surface can reflect light in two main ways.

Body reflection: light goes a little way into the material and scatters back out in all directions. This gives a soft, even look and is common on matte materials like clay and paper.

Surface reflection: light bounces straight off the surface. This is also called specular reflection, and it creates glossy highlights. It's the main kind of reflection on metals.

Scene settings

Scene Settings control the lighting, background colour and visual effects in the Render workspace.

In Fusion, your model always sits inside an environment dome: a large sphere with a picture wrapped around the inside. This picture is called an HDRI. It reflects in the surfaces of your model and lights the scene, much like a real room or outdoor setting would.

Video guide: Scene settings and HDRIs (YouTube) · Free HDRIs: Poly Haven

Scene settings: settings and environment library

The Scene Settings panel has two tabs. One holds the settings for the current scene, such as brightness, background and ground. The other is the environment library, where you choose a different environment to place your model in.

HDRI (High Dynamic Range Image)

An HDRI is a 360° image that's wrapped around your 3D model to light it and give it a background. "High dynamic range" means it stores a much wider range of brightness than a normal photo, from deep shadows to the bright sun. That's why it can light a scene realistically.

Rendering methods in Fusion

Fusion gives you two ways to render.

In-canvas render renders right in the main window using ray tracing. Ray tracing works out how light bounces around the scene, which makes the view look much more photo-realistic. Let it run for about 120 seconds before stopping it, then capture an image.

Render (local rendering) lets you choose your render settings, such as image size and quality, and then renders the image on your own computer.

Animation in Fusion

The Animation workspace lets you bring a design to life. You can:

  • create exploded views, where the parts separate so you can see how they fit together. Fusion can do this automatically, or you can do it by hand.
  • control exactly how each part or assembly moves

Animations are a good way to check a design and to explain it to others. For example, you can show how a product is assembled, or how to repair it.

The Animation interface

Three parts of the screen work together when you animate:

  • Storyboard: a collection of views and actions arranged along a timeline. It's like a script for your animation.
  • Action: one movement of a component at a certain point in time. To adjust when an action happens, change its start or finish time on the storyboard.
  • Timeline: the strip that shows time passing, where you place and line up actions.

Using animation: the workflow

Making an animation usually follows these steps:

  1. Open the Animation workspace.
  2. Create an animation.
  3. Explode the assembly.
  4. Edit the animation.
  5. Add annotations (labels and notes).
  6. Watch the animation.

You'll go through each of these in Activity 3.

Activity 3: Animation (part 3)

  1. Explode the model automatically. Rotate the model to the view you want, then double-click to select the top-level component. Click Transform > Auto Explode (All Levels).
  2. Adjust the explosion. Use the Auto Explode toolbar:
    • Move the slider to change how far apart the parts move (the explosion scale).
    • Choose One-Step Explosion to move all the parts at once.
    • Choose Sequential Explosion to move the parts one after another.

Activity 3: Animation (part 4)

  1. Set when the explosion starts and ends. On the timeline, move the green slider (start time) and the red slider (end time). Then click the tick on the toolbar to save the action.
  2. Time the camera movement. Drag the View bar on the timeline to change when the view changes.
  3. Go back to the start. Double-click the red-curtain icon to view the animation from the beginning.
